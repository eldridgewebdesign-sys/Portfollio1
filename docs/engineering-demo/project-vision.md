# Project Vision — The WebSharke Engineering Demo (`/engineering`)

> Every decision in this project — every camera move, every material, every line of copy — answers to
> one question:
>
> **"Would this make someone want WebSharke to build their website?"**
>
> If a choice doesn't serve that question, it gets cut. This document is binding on every specialist.

---

## 1. What we are building

A single page at `/engineering`. A visitor arrives, begins to scroll, and a thin silver laptop —
photoreal, sitting in warm studio light — takes itself apart. The lid lifts away. The heat pipes,
fan, SSD and memory rise off the board. The mainboard separates from the chassis. The camera moves
with intent, like a documentary lens, and short, plain sentences appear exactly when the thing they
describe is in front of you. At the end, the machine rests in a full exploded view — every part in
its place, named, understood — and one quiet line lands the point.

The visitor controls everything with the scroll wheel. Nothing plays without them. Nothing waits on
them. Scrolling backward reverses time. The page is the story; the scrollbar is the timeline.

## 2. Why a teardown (the narrative spine)

The WebSharke homepage makes one promise: **"Websites are complicated. Good thing we're not."**

This page dramatizes that promise. A laptop is the most complicated object on most desks — and here it
is, taken apart calmly, one layer at a time, until it makes sense. That is exactly what WebSharke does
with websites: take something intimidating apart and handle every piece, so the client doesn't have to.

This is why the teardown is the right subject and not an arbitrary tech flex:

- **The metaphor is the message.** Complexity, handled. Every reveal says "we know what's inside,
  and we're not afraid of it."
- **It demonstrates rather than claims.** The site's copy rules ban hype words. This page needs none —
  the proof is on screen. The demonstration *is* the sales pitch.
- **It's honest.** Real components, real names, real structure. No invented sci-fi hardware, no
  glowing circuits, no fake HUD. The confidence is in the calm.

Every specialist should be able to trace their choices back to this spine. A camera move that doesn't
help the visitor understand the machine is decoration. A text card that doesn't describe what's on
screen is noise.

## 3. Philosophy

1. **Show, don't claim.** The page contains almost no adjectives. It contains a laptop coming apart
   perfectly. Restraint in copy, ambition in craft.
2. **The visitor is the director.** Scroll is the only input. They set the pace, they can linger, they
   can rewind. Respecting their control is what separates an experience from an ad.
3. **Calm is confidence.** No urgency, no motion for its own sake, no competing focal points. One
   subject, beautifully lit, given room. The luxury-editorial principle from the site's design guide
   applies in 3D: whitespace and hierarchy carry the page; decoration is the exception.
4. **Physical, not digital.** The laptop obeys physical intuition — parts lift, tilt, settle, catch
   light. Materials read as aluminum, silicon, copper. Nothing teleports, nothing glows without a
   light source. The scene should feel photographed, not rendered.
5. **Handcrafted, provably.** A visitor should sense a human made a hundred small decisions. Specific
   easing, specific framing, specific words. Anything that could have been generated by a template
   gets rejected.
6. **Graceful everywhere.** The page must degrade with dignity: reduced motion, no JavaScript, no
   WebGL, small screens, slow connections. The fallback is a designed experience, not an apology.

## 4. Emotional goals (the arc)

The scroll should carry the visitor through four feelings, in order:

| Stage | Feeling | Produced by |
|---|---|---|
| Arrival | **Stillness** — "this is different" | A quiet, confident opening. One object, warm light, almost no UI. No pop-ups, no hero clutter |
| First movement | **Surprise** — "wait, I'm doing this" | The first scroll visibly moves the machine. The connection between hand and scene is instant and precise |
| The teardown | **Curiosity → understanding** | Each layer reveals something the visitor half-recognizes (fan, memory, board) and names it plainly. They feel smarter, not dazzled |
| The ending | **Trust** — "these people know what they're doing" | The full exploded view held in silence, then one plain closing line and a single, unhurried invitation |

The overall temperature is **warm, precise, and unhurried**. Not aggressive tech-noir. Not playful
cartoon. Think of a master watchmaker's bench, filmed beautifully.

## 5. Experience goals

- A first-time visitor scrolls to the end **unprompted** — pacing does that work, not instructions.
- At no point does the visitor wonder what to do or where they are in the story.
- Scrubbing feels engineered: reversing scroll reverses the scene exactly; there is no drift, no
  desync between scroll position and scene state.
- Motion sits on the scroll like a heavy, well-damped machine — smoothed, never laggy or floaty.
- Text is readable at the visitor's own pace; nothing critical animates away before it can be read.
- The page holds a fluid frame rate on a mid-range laptop and a two-year-old phone.
- The visitor leaves with two things: a memory of the experience, and a clear next step
  (start a project) offered once, at the end, without pressure.

## 6. Target audience

**Primary: small-business owners.** Non-technical. They arrive from the homepage or a shared link.
They will not name the technology; they will feel the difference between this and every template site
they've been quoted $2,000 for. The teardown must be legible to them — plain component names, plain
sentences, no jargon walls. The point they take away: *if WebSharke sweats this hard over a demo,
imagine my website.*

**Secondary: peers, developers, and judges.** People who *can* name the technology (Awwwards jurors,
other developers, agencies). For them the page must survive expert scrutiny: clean scroll binding, no
jank, honest 3D, no stock effects. They are the audience for the craft; the owners are the audience
for the story. The page must work for both at once.

## 7. Design language (guardrails, not the design)

The Creative Director owns the specifics inside these guardrails:

- **Typography is the site's system: Distillery Display (headings, caps-only face) + Playfair Display
  (body/UI), both vendored locally.** No third family, no CDN fonts. (Decision D-004.)
- **The environment is warm and physical.** The reference backdrop (`images/Laptop/interior.jpg`) sets
  the direction: soft beige studio, gentle daylight, honest shadows. The hardware provides the cool
  tones (silver aluminum, dark PCB, copper). Warm room, cool machine — that contrast is the palette's
  engine. No pure black, no pure white, no neon.
- **Light behaves like light.** One believable key light direction; shadows and reflections agree with
  it. Accent color, if any, must exist in the scene (anodized metal, copper, PCB solder) — never
  applied as UI glow.
- **UI is nearly invisible.** The scene is the interface. Text, progress indication, and the ending CTA
  are the only UI elements, and each must justify itself.
- **Composition gives the machine room.** Generous negative space, one focal point per beat,
  editorial framing. The site's design guide (brand-led, restrained, human-made) applies fully.
- **Copy is the owner's voice.** Plain, specific, a little dry. Short sentences. No exclamation marks.
  The banned-word list in `docs/design-guide.md` is law.

## 8. Inspiration

Held in `references.md` (Bruno Simon, Apple, Framework, iFixit, Awwwards) — each with what to learn
and what **not** to copy. The standing rule: references teach principles, never provide material.
This page must feel like nothing else, including its references.

## 9. Anti-AI-slop philosophy

The owner has rejected AI-flavored work before (numbered "01/02" eyebrow labels, glow-gradient
buttons, hype copy — see `docs/design-guide.md` history). This project holds the hardest line yet.

**Rejected on sight:**

- Generic gradients, floating glass cards, particle fields with no physical cause
- Meaningless buzzwords, fake statistics, invented testimonials
- Template layouts (hero / three cards / CTA), stock interactions, scroll-jacking that ignores intent
- Animations without narrative purpose; effects added because they look cool
- Empty whitespace used as filler rather than composition
- Sci-fi clichés: wireframe globes, HUD overlays, glowing circuit traces, matrix text, lens flares

**The test for every element:** name the sentence of the story it tells. If it has no sentence, cut it.

**The positive standard:** specificity. Specific component names (the fan is "the cooling fan", not
"advanced thermal system"). Specific camera reasons ("we move low and close here because the heat
pipes are best read in profile"). Specific easing choices with rationale. Specificity is what
handcraft looks like from the inside.

## 10. Quality standards

- **Documentation:** every specialist document must let a developer build without asking a question.
  If the Reviewer can ask "but what exactly happens when…?", the document is incomplete.
- **Motion:** every camera move and reveal has a stated purpose in the storyboard. Every duration and
  easing is specified, not "TBD".
- **Assets:** the reference images (`images/Laptop/`) are references only. Production assets are
  rebuilt to production quality per the Asset Director's pipeline — no upscaled slices, no shadows
  baked into the wrong layer (the predecessor's documented flaws are the checklist of what to fix).
- **Performance:** budgets are numbers (frame rate, payload, texture memory), set by the Technical
  Architect and enforced at review. "Fast enough" is not a budget.
- **Accessibility:** reduced-motion, no-JS, no-WebGL, keyboard, and screen-reader experiences are
  specified, designed, and reviewed — not discovered at the end.
- **Review:** average work is rejected. The Reviewer's bar is "would this compete for Site of the
  Day," not "is this fine."

## 11. Hard constraints (from the live site — non-negotiable)

1. Pure static deployment: vanilla HTML/CSS/JS, **no build step**. Vercel serves it; `cleanUrls` maps
   `/engineering` to a plain file. (Vendored ES modules are fine; a bundler is not.)
2. **No CDN requests.** Three.js, GSAP, fonts — all vendored locally. The predecessor page proved the
   value: nothing an ad-blocker or proxy can break. (Decision D-003.)
3. Typography: **Distillery Display + Playfair Display only**, self-hosted from `/fonts/`. (D-004.)
4. The existing `Animations/laptop-teardown` page stays live and untouched. (D-002.)
5. The page carries the WebSharke identity (logo, voice) and links back into the site; it is a page
   *of* the site, not a separate microsite.
6. Reference images stay where they are; production assets get their own home per the Asset Director.

## 12. Success criteria

The project succeeds when all of these are true:

1. A non-technical visitor, given the link, scrolls to the end without prompting and can say
   afterward what the page was about.
2. A developer's first reaction includes the question "how was this made?"
3. The owner would show it to a prospective client from their phone, on the spot, without caveats.
4. Every element on the page can be traced to a sentence of the story (the Section 9 test).
5. The page holds its frame-rate and payload budgets on mid-range hardware.
6. The reduced-motion and no-WebGL experiences read as designed, not broken.
7. Nobody — visitor, judge, or owner — can point at any element and plausibly say "AI made this."
8. It makes someone want WebSharke to build their website.
